We Shape Technology. Then Technology Shapes Us.

By Robert Pelot  •  September 20, 2026
A TOOL CAN BE DESIGNED BY PEOPLE AND STILL END UP CHANGING HOW PEOPLE THINK.

That tension—human control on one side, technological influence on the other—turned out to be more useful to me than choosing one theory and declaring it the winner.

I used to think about technology mostly in terms of what it could do.

That is natural when your background is in controls, troubleshooting, networking, cybersecurity, and systems administration. A tool has a purpose. A system has inputs and outputs. If it is designed well, it solves a problem.

A media-theory assignment forced me to look at technology from a different direction. Instead of asking only what a technology lets us do, the assignment asked what repeated use of that technology does to us.

Two Ideas That Seem to Contradict Each Other

Neil Postman argued that the form of a medium matters, not just the information placed inside it. The structure of a medium can change what attracts attention, what counts as persuasive, and what kinds of public conversation become normal.

Andrew Feenberg pushed from the other direction. Technology is created by people, embedded in social choices, and therefore can be redesigned. If people built the system, then people are not powerless in front of it.

At first those positions looked like alternatives: either technology shapes society, or society shapes technology. The more I thought about it, the less useful that either-or framing became.

The Feedback Loop Is the Interesting Part

We choose what to build. Designers choose defaults, interfaces, incentives, limits, notification patterns, ranking systems, and what information gets emphasized. Those choices are human decisions.

But once a system is used at scale, those decisions influence behavior. People adapt to the interface. They learn what gets rewarded. They change how they communicate, how long they pay attention, and even what they expect from other people and institutions.

That is why I no longer find it convincing to say that technology is “just a tool.” A hammer does not reorganize a person's social life. A platform used for hours every day can.

DESIGN CHOICES BECOME USER HABITS. USER HABITS BECOME CULTURE.

The people who choose the architecture of a system are not choosing every future outcome, but they are shaping the environment in which those outcomes become more or less likely.

The Designer Has More Responsibility Than the User Can See

One part of my original paper still stands out to me: the idea that the ability to shape technology is not distributed evenly. A user can choose whether to open an app, but a much smaller group decides how the app is structured, what it measures, what it recommends, and which behavior it rewards.

That matters because design decisions are often invisible once they become normal. A ranking algorithm looks like a list. A notification looks like a reminder. An autoplay feature looks like convenience. The engineering decision disappears behind the experience.

This is where technical work becomes an ethical responsibility. The question is not merely whether a feature works. It is also what behavior the feature encourages and what tradeoffs are being made on the user's behalf.

AI Makes the Question Harder, Not Different

Artificial intelligence makes this feedback loop easier to see because AI systems can sort, recommend, summarize, rank, and generate information at a scale that ordinary software never could.

The same questions still apply. What does the system optimize? What information is made prominent? What gets filtered out? How much control does the user actually have? What assumptions were built into the design?

I do not think the answer is to reject technology. My own life argues against that. Technology has opened doors for me, given me new ways to learn, and made possible many of the systems I now enjoy building.

The better answer is to stop pretending that convenience and neutrality are the same thing.

Education Is a Good Example

The same technology can make education more accessible while also changing what students expect learning to feel like. A tool that removes friction can be helpful. A tool that removes every reason to struggle with a difficult idea may remove part of the learning process too.

That does not make the tool bad. It means the design of the tool, and the way we choose to use it, matters.

The useful question is not “technology or no technology?” It is “what kind of relationship with technology are we designing?”

What I Carry Forward

The assignment started as an argument about Postman, Feenberg, media, and technological determinism. What I carried away from it was a much more practical idea.

  • Every system embeds choices, even when those choices are hidden behind a clean interface.
  • Users shape technology, but designers and organizations often have far more power to shape the environment.
  • A successful feature can still have consequences that were not part of the original requirement.
  • Convenience deserves the same kind of scrutiny as security, reliability, and performance.
  • The more widely a system is used, the more seriously its designers should think about the behavior it encourages.

That perspective now fits naturally beside the way I think about cybersecurity, automation, AI, and infrastructure. Technical systems are never only technical once people begin depending on them.

We build the system. Then we live inside the choices we built into it.

This article was adapted from my 2025 essay, “Evaluating Postman on Technology and Culture.” The original assignment compared Neil Postman's technological-determinist argument with Andrew Feenberg's view that technology can be socially shaped. This version keeps the ideas that connected most strongly with my own technical experience rather than reproducing the classroom paper.
