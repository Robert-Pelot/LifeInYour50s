When an Algorithm Decides Who Gets to See the Opportunity

By Robert Pelot  •  September 16, 2026
OPTIMIZED ≠ FAIR

A system can be working exactly as designed and still produce a result we should not accept.

Most people think of hiring bias as something that happens when one person looks at another person and makes a judgment.

But what happens when the judgment occurs before a human ever sees your name?

That question became personal for me while I was researching algorithmic bias for a computing ethics course. I was in the middle of trying to move into a new field later in life, sending out application after application and often hearing nothing back.

That experience did not prove that an algorithm was discriminating against me. But it made me much more interested in what happens upstream—before an application is reviewed, before an interview is offered, and in some cases before a person even sees that a job exists.

The more I read, the more one idea bothered me:

A system does not have to contain an explicit rule saying “exclude older workers” to produce an outcome that consistently favors younger ones.

The Algorithm May Never Reject You

One of the most important things I learned is that algorithmic bias does not have to look like a computer stamping REJECTED across a résumé.

Sometimes the system operates much earlier.

Advertising platforms decide which users are most likely to engage with a particular ad. To make that prediction, they can use large amounts of information about behavior, activity, interests, demographics, and patterns observed across many users.

From a business standpoint, that makes sense. Employers want their job postings shown to people who are likely to click on them.

But optimization has consequences.

If younger users historically interact with technology job ads more often, a system trained to maximize engagement may learn to show those ads to more younger users. No programmer has to write, “Do not show this job to people over 50.” The result can emerge from the objective the system was told to optimize.

That is what makes algorithmic bias difficult. The system can be working exactly as designed and still produce a result we should not accept.

Neutral Inputs Do Not Guarantee Neutral Outcomes

There is a comforting idea that computers are neutral because they do not have feelings, grudges, or prejudices.

That is true only in a very narrow sense.

Algorithms learn from data created by people and from patterns produced by society. They are also built around goals selected by people: maximize clicks, reduce cost, predict retention, rank applicants, or identify the users most likely to respond.

Those choices matter.

If the historical pattern contains bias, the model can learn the pattern. If the objective rewards engagement without measuring fairness, the system can become very good at engagement while quietly making access to opportunity less equal.

In other words, removing a biased decision-maker does not automatically remove bias from the decision process.

A Case That Made the Problem Hard to Ignore

The paper that started this article looked closely at Facebook's employment-ad targeting controversy.

Civil-rights and workers' organizations challenged practices that allowed employment advertising to be targeted in ways that could exclude people by characteristics such as age or gender. Facebook later changed parts of its advertising system and added restrictions intended to reduce discriminatory targeting in employment, housing, and credit advertising.

What interested me most was not simply that a platform had to change its rules.

It was the larger lesson: access to an opportunity can be shaped by a system that most users cannot inspect.

A person cannot apply for a job they never see.

And when an automated system decides who is likely to be interested, who is worth showing the ad to, or who resembles the people who responded in the past, those decisions deserve scrutiny even if the underlying code never mentions a protected characteristic.

Why This Felt Personal

I came to this subject as someone trying to make a career transition later in life.

After years of industrial and technical work, I went back to school, earned new credentials, and started applying for IT positions. I expected the transition to be difficult. What surprised me was how often the process felt like sending information into a black box.

Apply. Wait. Receive an automated rejection—or nothing at all.

It is tempting in that situation to look for one explanation: age, experience, résumé filters, applicant-tracking systems, market conditions, or simply a stronger candidate. The truth is that an applicant usually has no way to know.

That uncertainty is exactly why transparency matters.

I do not need every employer to explain every hiring decision in detail. But when automated systems influence who sees an opportunity, who advances, or who is filtered out, people should be able to understand the broad rules governing that process.

Otherwise, bias can hide behind the phrase, “the system decided.”

Efficiency Is Not the Same Thing as Fairness

This is one of the recurring problems I see as AI moves into more parts of everyday life.

We are very good at measuring whether a system is efficient.

Did it reduce recruiter workload? Did it increase click-through rates? Did it shorten the time to fill a position? Did it lower the cost per applicant?

Those are useful questions.

But they are not the only questions.

Who was never shown the opportunity? Which groups are consistently filtered out? Does the model reproduce an old pattern simply because that pattern exists in the training data? Can anyone audit the result? Can a person challenge an error?

A system can be efficient and unfair at the same time.

If fairness is not one of the things we measure, we should not be surprised when optimization ignores it.

What Responsible Use Should Look Like

The solution is not to abandon automation or pretend that human hiring was ever perfectly fair.

Technology can help organizations handle large applicant pools, find relevant skills, and reduce repetitive work. The problem begins when organizations treat automation as objective simply because it is automated.

For me, responsible use starts with a few basic ideas.

Systems that affect employment should be tested for unequal outcomes, not just technical accuracy. Organizations should know what data and objectives are driving the system. Automated decisions should be auditable. Humans should remain accountable for the results. And people affected by these systems should have some meaningful way to question outcomes that appear wrong.

That last point matters because an algorithm cannot accept responsibility.

The people and organizations deploying it have to.

The Lesson I Carried Away

I started researching algorithmic hiring bias because the subject felt close to my own experience.

I finished the project thinking about something broader.

AI does not have to be malicious to cause harm. It does not even have to be broken.

Sometimes the danger is a system doing exactly what it was optimized to do while nobody asks whether the optimization itself is fair.

That idea applies far beyond job advertising. It applies anywhere algorithms rank, recommend, filter, predict, or decide who gets attention.

As more of those decisions become automated, transparency and accountability cannot be optional extras added after a problem appears.

They have to be part of the design.

This article grew out of a computing ethics paper I wrote about algorithmic bias in employment advertising. I have intentionally reframed the personal portions of the original paper: my own job-search experience raised questions for me, but it does not by itself establish why any particular employer rejected an application.
