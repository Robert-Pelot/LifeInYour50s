Autonomy and Dignity: The Right to Choose End-of-Life Care

By Robert Pelot  •  March 5, 2025
CHOICE

Autonomy • dignity • informed decisions • safeguards

In debates over the control individuals have over their bodies, particularly when the matter at hand is healthcare, a key factor centers on autonomy. This aspect of autonomy is especially relevant to decisions about treatment near the end of life. In these difficult moments, patients should have the right to make decisions about their treatment, including refusing life-sustaining care and, where the law permits it, seeking physician assistance in dying, as long as they are capable of making an informed decision.

At the heart of this issue is the belief that people should control their bodies and health. Individuals facing terminal illnesses should have the right to refuse treatment, even if it means their illness will progress. If a person is of sound mind, they should have the autonomy to decide what happens to their body and how they want to spend their remaining time. This right should be respected even if the individual loses mental capacity later, when they have made their wishes known beforehand through tools such as an advance directive or living will.

In the case of physician-assisted dying, I believe doctors should be allowed to help terminally ill patients make end-of-life decisions where the law allows it. A physician's role is to provide the best care possible, and sometimes that care includes supporting a patient who chooses a peaceful and dignified death. Safeguards must be in place to ensure the person understands the consequences and is making the choice without coercion. Medical professionals should assess decision-making capacity, and counseling and palliative-care options should be available so patients can understand their choices.

The principal benefit of permitting people to decline medical treatment—and, in jurisdictions where it is lawful, to seek physician assistance in dying—is that it gives them more control over how they face the end of life. Conditions such as advanced cancer can produce suffering that is extremely difficult for the patient and for the people who love them. It is not an easy decision, but I believe it should ultimately remain with the individual, supported by appropriate medical care and clear safeguards.

Naturally, there are risks and difficult questions. A person could make a decision at a moment when a new treatment might soon appear, or when fear, depression, family pressure, or inadequate care is affecting judgment. Those concerns are reasons for strong safeguards, careful evaluation, good palliative care, and time for informed discussion—not, in my view, reasons to remove personal autonomy altogether.

Strong protections are necessary to ensure that these crucial decisions are made thoughtfully and voluntarily. There should be clear guidelines to establish decision-making capacity, informed consent, freedom from coercion, and an understanding of available alternatives. Above all, though, personal choice remains central because no two situations are identical.

To conclude, individuals diagnosed with terminal illnesses should have meaningful authority over what happens to them at the end of their lives. Deciding to stop treatment or pursue legally available physician-assisted dying is deeply personal. With safeguards in place to ensure a voluntary and informed decision, I believe people should be able to pursue dignity and relief from unnecessary suffering on their own terms.
