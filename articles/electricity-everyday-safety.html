What 30 Years Around Electricity Taught Me About Everyday Safety

By Robert Pelot  •  September 20, 2026
THE MOST DANGEROUS THINGS IN A HOUSE OFTEN LOOK COMPLETELY ORDINARY.

After decades around electrical and control systems, one lesson has never changed: familiarity can make people underestimate risk.

A wall outlet does not look dangerous.

It sits quietly at knee height, behind furniture, beside a lamp, or next to the place where a child plays. Most of the time it works exactly as expected, which is part of the problem. We become so accustomed to electricity that we stop seeing the hazard.

I spent more than three decades working around electrical systems, industrial controls, and energized equipment. I have seen experienced people make mistakes around electricity, and I have seen how quickly an ordinary situation can become serious. That experience changed the way I look at the outlets in my own home.

Experience Does Not Make Electricity Forgiving

Years ago, while I was working in a shipyard, I watched an experienced supervisor reach into a live distribution panel in a way he knew was unsafe. Within seconds, the mistake had consequences. He was thrown backward, injured, and badly shaken.

What stayed with me was not simply that electricity is dangerous. I already knew that. The lesson was that knowledge and experience do not create immunity from a moment of poor judgment.

If someone who understands the hazard can still make a dangerous mistake, a child who sees an outlet as one more interesting object to investigate has no way to judge the risk.

Then My Grandson Made the Risk Personal

That became more immediate when my grandson learned how to unplug things. Not long after that, he learned that plugs also go back in. To an adult, plugging in a lamp is routine. To a curious child, the exposed blades and slots are simply something to explore.

The Electrical Safety Foundation International says that more than 2,400 children under age 10 are treated in U.S. hospital emergency rooms each year for shock or burn injuries caused by tampering with wall outlets.

The number matters, but the larger lesson is that the hazard is ordinary, accessible, and easy to overlook.

Built-In Protection Is Better Than Remembering Every Time

One of the better ideas in modern residential electrical safety is the tamper-resistant receptacle, usually called a TRR. It looks much like a normal outlet, but internal shutters block access to the energized slots unless a normal plug applies pressure in the intended way.

That design appeals to me because the protection is built into the device. It does not depend on somebody remembering to put a removable cap back after using the outlet.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends using tamper-resistant receptacles when receptacles are being replaced. ESFI describes TRRs as a permanent form of protection against children inserting foreign objects into outlet slots.

BUILT-IN PROTECTION BEATS A PERFECT MEMORY.

Good safety design assumes that people will occasionally forget, become distracted, or make mistakes.

Pay Attention to the Outlet That Changes

Electrical problems do not always announce themselves dramatically. Sometimes the warning is a receptacle that feels unusually warm, a buzzing sound, discoloration, cracking, looseness, or a plug that no longer fits securely.

Those are not invitations to experiment. They are reasons to stop using the device and have the condition evaluated by a qualified electrician.

This is not a do-it-yourself wiring guide. The safest repair is the one that does not require an untrained person to discover the failure mode by touching energized parts.

Safety Is Usually a Layered System

The best safety systems rarely depend on one perfect action. Around children, that may mean tamper-resistant receptacles, keeping cords and plugs in good condition, arranging furniture so hazards are less accessible, teaching children what not to touch, and paying attention when an outlet starts behaving differently.

The same principle followed me from industrial controls into cybersecurity and systems work: do not bet everything on one safeguard. Use layers, assume people will make mistakes, and design the environment so one mistake does not immediately become a disaster.

What I Took Away

My original public-speaking assignment was about protecting children from wall outlets. Looking back at it now, I think the bigger lesson is about familiarity.

We live with electricity every day, so it becomes invisible. The outlet in the wall feels less like a piece of energized infrastructure and more like part of the furniture.

Thirty years around electrical systems taught me to resist that kind of familiarity. Respecting a hazard does not mean being afraid of it. It means understanding what it can do, building sensible protections around it, and recognizing when a situation has moved beyond what should be handled casually.

The safest system is the one that still protects us on the day somebody makes a mistake.

This article grew out of a public-speaking assignment on children and electrical outlets, combined with lessons from my earlier career in electrical controls and industrial systems.
