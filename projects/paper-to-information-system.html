From Paper Forms to an Information System

Legacy career project  •  Reconstructed from contemporaneous writing
PAPER → STRUCTURE → INFORMATION

I was doing systems analysis before I knew to call it systems analysis.

Long before I was studying Information Systems formally, I built one at work because the existing process was getting in the way.

The organization was tracking production figures, machinery maintenance information, and employee-related data on handwritten forms. The records contained useful information, but the process around them was slow, repetitive, difficult to analyze, and vulnerable to ordinary human mistakes.

The project began as a practical attempt to replace that paper workflow with a database-driven system. Looking back at it now, what interests me most is not the database itself. It is how many of the decisions I made then resemble the systems-analysis and user-centered design ideas I would later study in school.

Start by Understanding the Old System

I did not begin by designing tables or screens. I began with the existing records.

I gathered roughly a year of handwritten production history and worked through it to understand what people were actually recording, what the information meant, and what decisions depended on it. That gave me a picture of the real workflow rather than the workflow someone might describe from memory.

That distinction matters. A replacement system can be technically cleaner and still fail if it removes information people quietly depend on or forces them into a process that does not match the work.

Make the New System Feel Familiar

One design choice was deliberately conservative: the electronic forms resembled the paper forms the operators already knew.

I was not trying to impress anyone with a radically different interface. I wanted people to recognize what they were looking at, understand where information belonged, and move into the new system without having to relearn the job at the same time.

The important change happened underneath that familiar surface. Calculations that had previously been performed by hand could now be handled consistently by the system, reducing routine math mistakes and keeping results standardized.

Use Real History to Test the Replacement

The least glamorous part of the project became one of the most valuable.

I entered about a year of historical production data into the new system. At the time, it felt painfully repetitive. In hindsight, it gave me something much better than convenient test data: a known record of how the old process had behaved.

I could compare the database's calculations and outputs with actual historical records and work backward when something did not match. That made the previous year's paperwork a practical validation set for the replacement system.

Today I would describe that as test data, regression checking, and acceptance validation. At the time, it was simply the most convincing way I knew to answer the question: Does this thing actually produce the right result?

Design for the People Who Have to Use It

I built most of the system myself, but I did not design it entirely by myself.

Other supervisors tried the workflow and told me where navigation was confusing or where the system made a routine task harder than it needed to be. I changed the design in response.

That sounds obvious now, but it is an important lesson: the person building a system is usually the person least qualified to decide whether the interface is intuitive. The builder already knows how everything is supposed to work.

Actual users expose the assumptions.

The Bigger Change Was Not Paper to Digital

The most useful result was not that the records were stored electronically. It was that information that had been scattered across individual forms could now be examined together.

Production could be compared with staffing. Production changes could be viewed alongside machinery downtime. Trends that were difficult to recognize while looking at separate handwritten records became much easier to investigate once the information was structured in one place.

That is the difference between digitizing a form and building an information system.

A digital form merely replaces the paper. A useful information system changes what you can learn from the information after it has been collected.

Why I Include This Project Now

This is an older project, and I do not currently have the original database available to publish or demonstrate. I may eventually recover it from one of the older drives in my archive.

I still include the project because it connects two parts of my career that once seemed unrelated.

Years before returning to college for cybersecurity and Information Systems, I was already looking at a manual process, identifying failure points, preserving the parts users understood, automating repetitive work, validating a replacement against historical data, and changing the interface based on user feedback.

I did not have the vocabulary for all of it yet. The work came first; the formal names came later.
